Design directions

Four ways the product could look and feel. Every direction renders the same moment from step 1 of the demo script: Maya, 24 weeks pregnant, opens her week. Under each one, two small views show how it holds up for her partner and for a family with a 7 week old, because step 5 of the demo switches families and a look built only for pregnancy can fall apart there.

All names, values and dates are synthetic. Sources in the mocks show the format we will use; the real citations come from api/internal/rules.

What our users feel, and what the screen owes them

The brief from Denis: the app exists to lower the anxiety and mental load of busy parents who care a lot. Flo is the reference for how that feels. Flo works emotionally because it starts with reassurance, shows one thing at a time, explains why something matters, and lets you log a feeling in two taps. We borrow those habits, not its pink look.

What they feelWhere it comes fromWhat the design does about it
Fear of missing somethingScreening windows, vaccines and forms arrive from five places with different deadlines. Missing a glucose test or Tdap window feels like a failure as a parent.Open on "you are on track" and a count of what needs attention. Every item has a window, not a vague "soon". Done items stay visible so progress is felt.
Information overloadForums, apps and relatives all give different advice. Each search makes it worse.One plan for this week, short. Sources are folded behind each item so trust is one tap away but never in the way.
"Is this normal?"Body changes, sleep, mood swings and baby behaviour all look alarming the first time.Plain, kind language that names the data behind each point ("you slept under 6 hours on 3 of the last 4 nights"). Clear line between normal, worth asking about, and urgent.
Feeling unseen, and guiltyGeneric week-by-week content ignores her history. Partners are left out entirely and mothers carry the planning.Content visibly built from their data. Each parent has their own screen and accent colour; the shared plan shows who owns what.
ExhaustionLate pregnancy and newborn months mean broken sleep. Much of the use happens at night or one-handed.Soft colours that still pass WCAG AA, big targets, no red unless it is a real emergency. A night mode is worth having (direction D).
Panic in a real emergencyBleeding, severe headache, fewer kicks, thoughts of self-harm.A fixed, always-visible help path that looks different from everything else. Calm design must not hide it.

Rules every direction follows

Reassurance first

The first line on the home screen is a status sentence, not a list. "You are on track. Two things need you this week."

One focus

The week has one highlighted next step. Everything else is visible but quieter.

Every item says why

Trigger data, guideline, source and checked date sit behind each plan item. This is also how judges see that nothing is hardcoded.

Calm status scale

On track, upcoming, due, overdue use soft hues. Overdue is warm (clay or coral), never alarm red. Red is reserved for urgent symptoms and crisis.

Help is always one click away

The crisis and urgent-symptom entry is in every screen's frame, not in a menu. It opens a fixed screen with hotlines, never generated text.

Two parents, two colours

Mom and Partner each get an accent, used for ownership in the shared plan and the shift handoff. Neither is pink or baby blue.

Steady

calm clinic, warm paper

A clear, quiet dashboard: the plan in the middle, the reason behind the selected item on the right. It feels like a good private clinic portal that someone made kind. Built for desktop judges: personalisation and sources are visible without clicking.

B

Letter

a weekly letter from someone who knows you

The weekly plan is written and laid out like a short letter, with sources as margin notes. The AI plan becomes the hero, and it reads as personal because it is prose about her week, not a feed. Data screens (passport, labs) use the same paper and rules but switch to a plain sans for tables.

Recommendation

Build A (Steady) as the base, take the "today" focus card and stage shapes from C, and write the plan in B's voice.

Judges score working end to end and the realness of the data at 40%, on a laptop. A's layout puts the reason, the data and the source next to every plan item without a click, and it extends to the passport, labs, booking and pricing screens with no new patterns, which matters with one frontend developer and a noon deadline. On its own it risks feeling like a dashboard, so we add C's single focus card on top of the plan, a soft generated shape per stage to make the family switch obvious, and B's letter-like tone for the AI plan text (that part is a prompt and copy decision more than a visual one). D becomes a night mode later if there is time; it is a token swap once A's tokens exist.

Decisions I need from Denis or Dayana before building:

  • The product name. Everything here says "Parenting Planner", the placeholder from web/src/lib/product.ts; the logo and wordmark wait on it.
  • Whether each parent gets their own accent colour. I recommend it, since it makes ownership and handoffs readable, but it fixes the palette early.
  • The track. docs/requirements.md says Skills and "learning through practice", while docs/product.md still lists the track as open and pitches a plan and data hub. If it is Skills, the design needs a practice or lesson surface that none of these mocks show.
